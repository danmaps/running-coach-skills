# Running Journal

Read and apply the [shared coaching rules](references/coaching-contract.md) before proceeding.

The journal is the athlete-owned record of what training felt like and what it taught. Device data supplies the objective record; the plan remains the source of truth for accepted targets and session status. The journal adds the context a coach needs before proposing a change: feel, body notes, sleep, life demands, fueling outcomes, and repeated lessons.

## Set up and collaboration

1. Read the profile and plan, then apply the shared journal-preference rules before reading or writing the existing journal in the private working folder. If the athlete wants a journal and none exists, create `journal.md` from the [journal template](assets/journal.template.md). Do not make journaling a condition for coaching.
2. Honor the profile setting: `off` skips journal work unless explicitly requested now; `on request` allows reading for coaching but requires an explicit request for entries or journal-specific prompts; `after runs` permits entry updates during requested debriefs. An explicit journal request overrides the setting for that operation only. Respect freeform notes and leave the standing preference unchanged unless asked to change it.
3. Draft objective parts from confirmed activity and source data only. Never ask the athlete to retype distance, duration, route, or conditions that an accessible source already establishes.
4. Ask for no more than three material subjective details at a time. Good prompts are: how it felt, whether anything hurt or felt unusual, and the one takeaway. Ask about fueling, sleep, or life context when they are relevant to the session or a visible pattern.
5. Preserve the athlete's words for reports and takeaways. You may place them into the entry structure, but do not polish away meaning or turn a tentative report into a stronger claim. Label agent interpretation separately.
6. Upsert entries by local date plus session ID and canonical activity ID when known. Reopening or correcting an entry updates the same record; it must not create a duplicate.
7. A missing entry is missing context, not noncompliance. Never shame the athlete or block other coaching because they did not journal.

## What should be tracked

These are the core fields because each one can change a later coaching decision.

| Detail | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Entry key: local date, session ID, and canonical activity ID when known | Links the journal to the accepted prescription and recorded activity without duplicating either one. |
| Objective snapshot: session type, actual distance or duration, route, terrain, and conditions | Gives subjective notes their context. Mark the source and leave unavailable fields unknown. |
| Effort and feel, using the athlete's words plus an optional 1–10 scale | Subjective load can reveal strain or ease that pace and heart rate do not establish. Trends matter more than one rating. |
| Body notes: sensation, location, when it appeared, optional 1–10 severity, and whether it changed during or after the run | Creates a dated pattern instead of relying on memory. This is observation only, never a diagnosis or medical severity assessment. |
| Sleep and energy before the run | Explains performance variation and helps distinguish ordinary fatigue from a change in fitness. Use reported or accessible data; do not invent either. |
| Life context that affected training | Work, family demands, travel, schedule disruption, and similar context explain missed or altered sessions and help fit future plans to the athlete's real week. Keep it as brief as the athlete wants. |
| Fueling and hydration: timing, products or foods, approximate amounts, and gastrointestinal or energy response | Turns long-run and race experiments into a personal playbook based on what demonstrably worked. Record failures without treating one attempt as a permanent rule. |
| Shoes and notable gear | Connects feel and body notes to equipment and supplies the shoe history without making the athlete calculate mileage manually. |
| Intention compared with experience | Shows whether the session accomplished its purpose and whether the prescription itself, rather than the athlete, needs adjustment. |
| One-line takeaway | Preserves the lesson in the athlete's terms. Repeated takeaways become planning evidence; one-off impressions stay provisional. |

## What may be tracked

Use these when they are relevant or the athlete finds them useful. None is required.

| Detail | Why it may help |
| --- | --- |
| Best and hardest moments | Retains positive evidence and motivation, not only problems. |
| Clothing compared with weather | Builds a personal guide for similar conditions. |
| Route notes: water, toilets, footing, lighting, access, hazards, or closures observed | Improves future route choices; label the observation date and do not present it as a current access check. |
| Food timing, caffeine, or other deliberate experiments | Helps isolate variables when fueling or energy changes. Change one major variable at a time when experimenting. |
| Training partners, setting, and headspace | Can explain adherence, enjoyment, and perceived effort. |
| Questions for the coach or decisions to revisit | Keeps open loops visible for the next review instead of burying them in chat. |
| Weight or cycle notes | Only when the athlete explicitly chooses to track them. Do not solicit them as a default or comment casually on changes. |

## Standing sections

Maintain these in the journal only from entries that are actually present. Date or link every distilled claim to its source entries.

- **Body-note rollup:** recurring sensations by location, first and last seen, and current athlete-reported status. Surface patterns; do not diagnose causes.
- **Fueling playbook:** what worked, what did not, and what remains untested for long runs and races. Race preparation should read this before proposing race fueling.
- **Conditions and clothing guide:** combinations the athlete has tested and what they would repeat or change.
- **Route notes:** dated observations that can inform, but never substitute for, a current route access and conditions check.
- **Lessons learned:** takeaways repeated or confirmed across entries. Keep the athlete's wording where practical.
- **Race reflections:** one post-race record of preparation, pacing, fueling, logistics, and what to repeat or change. Write it from the athlete's report and confirmed activity data.
- **Open questions:** decisions, experiments, and uncertainties to revisit in a check-in or plan revision.

## Use the journal for planning

1. When journal reading is permitted by the shared preference rules, before plan building, plan revision, a weekly check-in, a debrief, or race preparation, read recent entries relevant to the period and any standing section that bears on the decision.
2. Cite the specific journal evidence when it influences a proposal: dates, repetition, and the athlete's own report. Separate that evidence from device data and from your interpretation.
3. Use journal patterns to propose a reconciled change through the owning skill, usually plan-builder for targets or weekly-check-in for recurring patterns. A journal note never changes a future target by itself; follow the shared acceptance and adjustment-log rules.
4. If journal reports conflict with device data, preserve both. Normal device data cannot dismiss pain, illness, or unusual symptoms, and a subjective report does not rewrite confirmed distance or time.
5. When one entry suggests an experiment, state the single variable to test and where the result will be recorded. Do not turn an anecdote into a standing rule until later entries support it.

## Output

State what entry or standing section was created or updated, which subjective fields remain unreported, and any planning implication that is supported. Keep routine updates brief.

## Guardrails

- The journal is private athlete data. Do not place it in the public repository, quote it to third parties, or include exact home locations or unnecessary health details in shared output.
- Body notes are observations, not diagnoses. Follow the shared symptom exception immediately when reports warrant it; do not wait for a journal trend.
- Never invent feel, symptoms, fueling, sleep, or life context. Unknown stays unknown.
- Do not let journaling become homework. If the process is burdensome, reduce prompts and keep only the fields the athlete values.
